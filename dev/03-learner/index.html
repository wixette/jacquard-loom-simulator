    The machine

    Joseph Marie Jacquard's head (Lyon, 1801–1805) let a chain of punched cards choose which warp threads rise for each throw of the shuttle, so one weaver could weave any figure without a draw boy. This is a typical single-lift head of the later 1800s, not Jacquard's own: the cards press on horizontal needles; each needle holds a vertical hook; a rising griffe of knives lifts the hooks whose needles found a hole, and with them their warp threads.

    One convention

    A hole is a 1: its warp end is lifted over the weft. A blank pushes its needle, and its hook off the knife, so the end stays down.

    A program, but not a computer

    The chain is a fixed sequence of instructions read in order, and the loom repeats a loop of cards for as long as it runs. That is why Babbage borrowed the cards for his Analytical Engine. But the loom cannot choose: it never branches on what it has woven, it does no arithmetic, and it writes nothing back. Ada Lovelace put it this way: the Engine "weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves".

    What is simplified

    • The timing within a pick is estimated; the order of events is from the period textbooks.
    • Stepping back unweaves the last row exactly. The real reverse cord turns the cylinder back, and the next lift weaves the previous card again.
    • A pushed hook is drawn moving sideways above the grate; the cylinder's swing is drawn as a slide; the harness is shortened; the front view is schematic and the side view only roughly to scale.
    • The picture helper's damask uses one 8-end satin for figure and ground; floats can grow a thread or so where blocks meet.
    • In 3D, one of each part is shown (the selector shows the followed hook's state on hook 1's parts); the cylinder's batten and the loom's batten slide rather than swing; the S-iron is left out; the loom's frame and beams are estimated.
