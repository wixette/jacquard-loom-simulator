# Notes: history, versions and surviving machines

**Answers:** (1) the lineage from Bouchon to Verdol and what each step added; (2) what the first Jacquard head (c. 1804–05) looked like; (3) which original or reconstructed machines survive, where, and which are demonstrated; (4) the woven portrait, Lovelace's Note A, Babbage's cards and Hollerith; (5) which machine the simulator should model.

## 1. Lineage: Bouchon → Falcon → Vaucanson → Jacquard → Breton → later heads

### Takeaway
The parts of the Jacquard head were not new in 1804–05. Perforated paper (Bouchon 1725), a chain of cards on a square prism (Falcon 1728), and a rising griffe with automatic card motion (Vaucanson 1745–46) all came first. Jacquard's contribution was a simple, self-acting, cheap combination that weavers could actually use. A careful 1873 German history argues that his famous 1801 patent covers a *different*, earlier machine, and that the card machine dates most probably to 1805, with no patent of its own.

### Cited findings

**Basile Bouchon, 1725.**
- A band of perforated paper is pressed by hand against a row of horizontal wires (needles). The wires facing blank paper are pushed forward, which engages loops on vertical cords with a comb-like rack below. One worker still did all this by hand on a draw loom [BARLOW-1878 p.140–141, quoting WILLIS-1855; POSSELT-1888 "History", copied almost word for word from the same passage].
- Barlow's chronology: "1725. M. Bonchon [*sic*, OCR/typo for Bouchon] invented the application of perforated paper for working the draw-loom, being the origin of the Jacquard Machine" [BARLOW-1878 p.33].
- Barlow says nothing is known about Bouchon or Falcon as people [BARLOW-1878 p.146].

**Jean-Baptiste Falcon, 1728.**
- Falcon "substituted a chain of cards, and a square prism (known as the cylinder) in lieu of the band of paper of Bonchon" [BARLOW-1878 p.141; same in POSSELT-1888].
- Kohl: Falcon's loom had hooks with needles and "ein Prisma mit Karten" ("a prism with cards"). It was not self-acting: the prism "von einem zweiten Arbeiter oder einem Kinde gedreht und hin- und herbewegt werden musste" ("had to be turned and moved back and forth by a second worker or a child"). "Die Kleinheit des vierseitigen Falcon'schen Prisma und die geringe Breite der Kartenstreifen" ("the small size of Falcon's four-sided prism and the narrow card strips") allowed only small patterns [KOHL-1873, Einleitung].
- Barlow credits Falcon with placing the needles "in tiers, as well as in rows", that is, a 2-D needle block. He calls it "very remarkable" that this was invented in 1728 and then "remained nearly a century without any practical result" [BARLOW-1878 p.150].
- The Science Museum's model of Falcon's loom (1963-379) describes it as "the first machine of any kind to be controlled by punch cards". The cards are tied into an endless chain, and a draw boy still presents the cards to the horizontal wires [SMG-1963-379]. *This is a museum's claim, not a documented first; see §6.*

**Jacques de Vaucanson, 1745–46.**
- Vaucanson "suppressed altogether the cumbrous tail-cards of the draw loom, and made the loom completely self-acting". He put the pierced paper on "a large pierced cylinder, which travelled backwards and forwards at each stroke, and revolved through a small angle by ratchet work". "He also invented the rising and falling griffe" [BARLOW-1878 p.141, quoting WILLIS-1855].
- Barlow's chronology: Vaucanson "applied the griffe to M. Falcon's invention, and placed the apparatus on the top of the loom in the position now occupied by the Jacquard machine" [BARLOW-1878 p.34].
- Kohl: Vaucanson *gave up* Falcon's prism for a large drum ("eine umfängliche Mustertrommel", "a large pattern drum"), "dieser gerade nicht glückliche Tausch" ("this not exactly happy exchange"). He abandoned the project because the loom was heavy and complicated and because weavers opposed it after his 1745 announcements [KOHL-1873, Einleitung].
- CNAM record, inv. **00017-0001**: "Métier à tisser les étoffes façonnées de Vaucanson destiné à remplacer l'ancien métier à la tire" ("Vaucanson's loom for figured fabrics, intended to replace the old draw loom"). Dated **1746**, entered the collection in **1783**, full-scale object, **330 × 266 × 277 cm**, wood, cardboard, brass, lead and textile. Cams drive the mechanism, shuttle and batten, and an endless screw takes up the cloth. It was "never applied industrially" [CNAM-00017-0001].
- Date disagreement: CNAM says 1746 [CNAM-00017-0001]; Barlow, Posselt and Willis say 1745 [BARLOW-1878]; English Wikipedia's lead says 1740, citing Razy 1913 p.120 [WIKI-JACQUARD-MACHINE]. Grace's Guide gives "1745–48" and suggests the perforated-drum mechanism may date from as late as 1775 (snippet) [GRACES-VAUCANSON].
- Jacquard and Vaucanson's loom: at the Conservatoire, Jacquard saw "die Ueberbleibsel" ("the remains") of Vaucanson's machine, which had never been made to work, and restored it. He told the Société d'encouragement that one loom on this system would cost no less than 10,000 francs and would weave only small patterns, slowly [KOHL-1873 p.12]. In his own account (1831–32 Commons evidence by John Bowring, reprinted by Barlow), Jacquard produced his mechanism by "improving upon a model by Vaucanson" [BARLOW-1878 p.145].

**Joseph-Marie Jacquard: the 1801 patent, 1804 and 1805.** *This is the most disputed point.*
- Kohl lists the dates given for the invention as 1799, 1801, 1808 and 1812, and sets out to settle them [KOHL-1873, Einleitung].
- The patent is a ten-year brevet d'invention dated **23 December 1801**, "pour une machine destinée à suppléer le tireur de lacs, dans la fabrication des étoffes brochées et façonnées" ("for a machine intended to replace the cord-drawer [draw boy] in making brocaded and figured fabrics"), printed in *Brevets d'invention*, vol. IV [KOHL-1873, footnote, OCR-repaired].
  - Elsewhere Kohl gives the grant as **2 January 1802** [KOHL-1873 p.11].
  - French web sources give 23 Dec 1801, 18 Jan 1801, and "2 nivôse an IX" (= 23 Dec 1800) (snippets) [SEARCH-BREVET-1801].
- Kohl's conclusion, from the patent text and the Société d'encouragement *Bulletins* of 1806 (pp.205, 232) and 1807 (p.193):
  - The weaving machine of 1799–1801 was "keineswegs die allbekannte Maschine seines Namens, sondern die … Latzenzug-Maschine" ("by no means the well-known machine bearing his name, but the … lacs-drawing machine").
  - The card machine was made after his return from Paris in 1804, "am wahrscheinlichsten im Jahre 1805" ("most probably in the year 1805"). The evidence is that in June 1806 he was already notified of an imperial premium of 50 francs per machine delivered.
  - "Da sich weder in den Brevets d'invention noch in anderen Schriften eine Patenturkunde über die Jacquard-Maschine vorfindet" ("since no patent document for the Jacquard machine is found either in the Brevets d'invention or in other writings"), Jacquard probably never meant to patent it [KOHL-1873, Einleitung].
- Millénaire 3 (Grand Lyon) also says the 1801 device was not the card mechanism but "une mécanique avec un nombre limité de pédales" ("a mechanism with a limited number of pedals"). Geneawiki says the first machine "ne fonctionne pas" ("did not work") (both snippets) [SEARCH-BREVET-1801].
- Barlow mentions an 1801 machine of a different principle (a harness-division system), "the model of which is preserved in the Conservatoire des Arts et Metiers" [BARLOW-1878 p.127]. Barlow also says Jacquard "in 1801 had completed it" and won a bronze medal at the Paris national exposition [BARLOW-1878 p.140], so he does not separate the two machines.
- The bronze medal at the September 1801 Paris exhibition was for the lacs-drawing machine [KOHL-1873 p.11]. The Musée des Tissus also says it was for a mechanism "qui supprime le travail de l'ouvrier appelé tireur de lacs" ("that removes the work of the worker called the cord-drawer") [MDT-2014-DP].
- **2 February 1804:** the Société d'encouragement pour l'industrie nationale gave Jacquard its large gold medal and 3,000 francs (for the net-making machine), plus a post at the Conservatoire [KOHL-1873 p.11]. Posselt says this came "from the London Society" [POSSELT-1888], which looks like a mix-up with the London Society of Arts' net-machine prize mentioned in Jacquard's own account [BARLOW-1878 p.144].
- Napoleon.org (snippet): in January 1803 the First Consul asked Carnot to install Jacquard at the Conservatoire with his machine [NAPOLEON-ORG].
- Jacquard returned to Lyon "noch im Jahre 1804" ("still in 1804") and finished his main invention there [KOHL-1873 p.12].
  - In 1806 he received an annuity of 3,000 francs and had to transfer his invention to the city of Lyon [POSSELT-1888].
  - Barlow gives the pension as 1,500 francs (£60) plus 50 francs (£2) per machine sold [BARLOW-1878 p.140].
  - Bowring's account gives "a pension of 1000 crowns" [BARLOW-1878 p.145].
  - *These amounts disagree.*
- **1819:** gold medal at the 5th Exposition des produits de l'industrie française "pour des perfectionnements de la machine à faire les étoffes façonnées" ("for improvements to the machine for making figured fabrics"), followed by the Légion d'honneur. The medal, by Raymond Gayrard, is in the Musée des Tissus, inv. MT 18790 [MDT-2014-DP].
- Willis's judgement (1855, quoted by Barlow): "The merit of Jacquard is not, therefore, that of an inventor, but of an experienced workman, who, by combining together the best parts of the machines of his predecessors in the same line, succeeds for the first time in obtaining an arrangement sufficiently practical to be generally employed" [BARLOW-1878 p.141]. Kohl takes the opposite view: Jacquard's machine "alle vorhergehenden an Einfachheit übertraf" ("surpassed all its predecessors in simplicity") [KOHL-1873, Einleitung].
- Riots and the burned machine: Bowring quotes Jacquard as saying the Conseil des Prud'hommes "broke up his machine in the public place; the iron … was sold for iron and the wood for wood" [BARLOW-1878 p.145]. Rojas (2012) says the public-burning story "n'a jamais existé" ("never happened") and traces it to 1833–34 [ROJAS-2012].
- Spread of the machine:
  - "about 11,000" Jacquard looms in France by 1812 (Aumasson 1987, via French Wikipedia) [FRWIKI-METIER-JACQUARD].
  - "over 30,000" machines in Lyon at Jacquard's death in 1834 [POSSELT-1888].
  - It reached England about 1816–23: Hale saw it in 1816 and Stephen Wilson had looms at Streatham. Wilson told a Lords committee in 1823 of a shawl woven with 1,200 cords, and of a pattern then on the loom "with 7000 leashes". Coventry's first Jacquard came in 1823 or 1824 [BARLOW-1878 p.142–144].
  - The Science Museum's Spitalfields loom has a Jacquard head dated about 1820–34 [SMG-1914-469].

**Jean-Antoine Breton, 1815.** *Snippets only; the primary text was not read.*
- A five-year brevet de perfectionnement dated **28 February 1815**, to "Breton, mécanicien à Lyon … pour un perfectionnement fait au mécanisme dit à la Jacquard, destiné à remplacer le tireur de lacs" ("Breton, mechanic in Lyon … for an improvement to the so-called Jacquard mechanism, intended to replace the cord-drawer"). An addition followed in 1817 (snippet) [GENEAWIKI-JACQUARD; HOI-441].
- What Breton changed:
  - Geneawiki (snippet): a "pièce coudée" ("angled/elbow piece") that pressed the card evenly onto the square prism.
  - English sources: he fixed the card-feed faults that had limited sales, and sales rose after 1815.
  - French Wikipedia credits Breton, a "menuisier" (joiner), as co-developer in 1801 [FRWIKI-METIER-JACQUARD].
- **Not confirmed:** what part Breton changed. The often-repeated idea that he made the needle–hook action reliable could not be checked against the patent text.

**Later heads.**
- **Double lift.** Posselt names the "Double Lift Single Cylinder" and "Double Lift Double Cylinder" machines as modifications of the single lift. The double lift has two hooks per needle and two griffes working in opposition, so "the shed is never entirely closed". He calls it faster and gentler on the warp, with 8 against 14 warp movements for a 7-pick float in an 8-shaft satin [POSSELT-1888, "Modifications of the Single Lift Jacquard Machine", printed pp.67–71].
- **Single lift.** The common hand-loom head is single lift: "raising and lowering of plunger and griffe for each pick" [POSSELT-1888 "The Plunger"].
- **Verdol.**
  - CNAM shows a "Mécanique Jacquard type Verdol" dated **1883** [ROJAS-2012].
  - Its principle is "substituant le papier sans fin aux cartons enlacés" ("putting endless paper in place of laced cards"), with a strong paper glued into a continuous loop (snippets of Lyon heritage inventory records).
  - The Société anonyme des mécaniques Verdol was at the Croix-Rousse from 1895. It made Verdol heads in "simple lève, lève et baisse, … à double cylindre" ("single lift, lift-and-drop, … double cylinder") versions [AURA-VERDOL].
  - **Not confirmed:** the "fine pitch" (small needle spacing) and the exact patent year. I found no primary source for Verdol's patent.
- Kohl (1873) traces the many variants before Verdol: iron heads, dobbies, double machines, and paper or sheet-metal cards [KOHL-1873, Theil II].

### Inferences
- The 1801/1804/1805 dispute has a likely cause. The 1801 patent and medal were for the pedal "lacs-drawing" machine. The card head came after the Paris stay (1803–04), most likely in 1805, and was rewarded in 1806 by pension and premiums rather than by a patent. "1804" is the conventional date because it is easy to remember and is used by the Science Museum [SMG-1867-47; SMG-1963-379].
- The two dates Kohl gives for the patent (23 Dec 1801 and 2 Jan 1802), and the web's 23 Dec 1800, probably come from converting a nivôse date of the Republican calendar: 2 nivôse an X = 23 Dec 1801, and 2 nivôse an IX = 23 Dec 1800. This is my reasoning, not something a source says.
- Posselt's history copies Barlow and Willis almost word for word, so it is not an independent source for the Bouchon–Falcon–Vaucanson story. The real root is Willis's 1855 exhibition report, which describes the nine Marin models then shown and later kept at the CNAM.

### Gaps
- The INPI brevet scans (1801 Jacquard; 1815 Breton) were not reached. INPI's historical patent base (bases-brevets19e.inpi.fr) was not searched in this pass.
- Charles Ballot, *L'introduction du machinisme dans l'industrie française* (1923), is not on archive.org under that search. Gallica was not searched. Razy 1913 (Musée des Tissus models catalogue) was not read.
- Willis 1855 (the root English source) was not opened directly, only through Barlow.
- No primary source for Verdol's patent year or needle pitch.

## 2. The original Jacquard head (c. 1805): hooks, prism, cards, mounting, drive

### Takeaway
The best period description I found is Kohl's 1873 account of the head "in seiner ursprünglichen Form" ("in its original form"), taken from the *Dictionnaire technologique*. It describes a wooden frame with **8 rows** of wire hooks lifted by **8 iron knives**, a **four-sided (square) prism** with a 4-bar lantern turned a quarter turn per card by two catches (forward or back), brass spiral springs, horn or wooden pegs, and probably two reserve rows. **No source I read gives the hook count of the very first machine.** The numbers in detail come only from later machines: Posselt's 1888 200-hook head and Barlow's 1878 400-hook hand-loom head.

### Cited findings

**Kohl's "original form" (after the *Dictionnaire technologique*, vol. XI; OCR reads "Bd. XL"):**
- **Frame and hooks.** A wooden frame A with a perforated wooden bottom board a. On it stand hooks ("Platinen") B of iron wire with a hooked bend.
- **Griffe.** The knife box C slides in grooves in the frame posts, lifted by a lever D. Its iron end cheeks carry "die 8 eisernen Messer oder Stäbe" ("the 8 iron knives or bars") that form the grate (griffe) [KOHL-1873 p.32].
- **Batten.** A swinging frame ("Lade", batten) F hangs from screw points in the top of the frame. A roller on an arm of the knife box runs in an S-shaped iron "Presse", so lifting the griffe swings the batten and prism away from the needles [KOHL-1873 p.32].
- **Prism.** "Das Prisma, der von Jacquard zugefügte Haupttheil der Maschine" ("the prism, the main part Jacquard added") has drilled holes "an seinen 4 Seiten" ("on its 4 sides"), matching the card holes and the horizontal rows of needles [KOHL-1873 p.32].
- **Needles and springs.** Each hook passes through an eye in its needle. The rear needle loops are held by a vertical pin and pressed by "Spiralfedern … von Messing" ("brass spiral springs") in a spring box. Wooden laths in the lower hook rows stop the hooks from turning [KOHL-1873 p.33].
- **Pegs and turning.** Conical horn or wooden pegs near the ends of each face engage the card holes. "Für das vierseitige Prisma wird daher zum Vorlegen einer neuen Karte eine Viertelumdrehung desselben bedungen" ("for the four-sided prism, presenting a new card therefore needs a quarter turn"). A lantern of 4 bars and a catch ("Wendehaken oder Hund", "turning hook or dog") on the frame do the turning. A second catch turns it backwards, chosen with a cord over a pulley. A spring-loaded crutch ("Krücke") limits each move to a quarter turn [KOHL-1873 p.33].
- **Reserve rows.** "Der gegebenen Zeichnung nach enthält das Prisma … auch schon zwei Reihen Löcher für Reserveplatinen" ("according to the drawing the prism already has two rows of holes for reserve hooks"), used for selvedges, signals, shuttle boxes and so on [KOHL-1873 p.33].
- **Wood and iron versions.** Kohl's next plate (Taf. V) shows a wooden Jacquard head, with frame, griffe, lever, batten and hooks of wood, and an iron one [KOHL-1873 p.34].

**Rows.**
- English Wikipedia says Jacquard's machine "contained eight rows of needles and uprights" where Vaucanson had a double row, with no citation [WIKI-JACQUARD-MACHINE]. This agrees with Kohl's 8 knives.
- Posselt says the needle arrangements "most used are 4, 8, and 12 rows high", with one griffe knife per row [POSSELT-1888].

**Prism shape.**
- Square (four-sided) in Kohl [KOHL-1873], Barlow ("square prism", Falcon) [BARLOW-1878 p.141] and Posselt ("a quadrilateral and perforated cylinder") [POSSELT-1888].
- Posselt and Barlow both note it is "technically called the cylinder" [POSSELT-1888; BARLOW-1878 p.150].
- I found no source for a hexagonal or other prism on early heads. Later fine-pitch heads are outside what I read.

**Mounting.** The head sits "on the top of the loom, in the place of the pulley box" of the draw loom, the position Vaucanson had chosen [BARLOW-1878 p.142].

**Drive.**
- The weaver works the griffe with a treadle. In Jacquard's account the prefect "desired him to strike with his foot" (that was the net machine) [BARLOW-1878 p.145].
- On the Science and Industry Museum's teaching loom, the weaver works the mechanism with a foot lever [SMG-Y1973.43].
- **Not found:** an early drawing with the treadle-to-lever linkage dimensions.

**Before and after.**
- Before the Jacquard, the figure shed was "generally drawn every four shoots". Wilson's Jacquard loom drew it "every shoot" [BARLOW-1878 p.144].
- Jacquard's own patent claimed his earlier (1790) machine was on "beinahe 4000 Stühlen" ("almost 4,000 looms") in Lyon by 1801 [KOHL-1873, Einleitung]. That is the pre-card machine.

**Numbers for a later standard head: Posselt's "common 200 Jacquard", drawn at 1/4 scale [POSSELT-1888]:**

**Barlow's "ordinary 400-needle machine — such as is in common use in silk hand-loom weaving" [BARLOW-1878 p.149–150]:**
- The usual sizes are 300, 400, 600 and 900. The 300 heads are used on power looms for figured stuff goods, and "the higher numbers are used on hand looms for weaving figured silks".
- "Machines intended for hand-loom weaving are usually made of wood, but for power looms iron is used."
- The 400-needle machine has "more than that number of needles in it", that is, reserves.
- Griffe bars are of "strong hoop iron with feather edges". Springs are spiral, of thin brass wire, in a removable spring box. Needles are "about ¼ in. apart" (the OCR reads "\ in."; check the scan).
- With up to 100–200 cards they fall into a curved tin frame. Larger sets fold in a "festoon", with a wire about 2 in longer than the cards at every 20th card [BARLOW-1878, the chapter's construction pages].

### Inferences
- A model of "the 1804 original" would have to be pieced together. Kohl's original-form description gives the layout: 8 rows, square prism, wooden frame, single lift, two reserve rows. The column count, card size and frame height would have to be borrowed from later wooden hand-loom heads. Kohl's plate (from the *Dictionnaire technologique*) is the best drawing to look for.
- The 8-row hook block with square prism, lantern, hammer and two catches stayed the same from about 1805 to Posselt's 1888 head. The machine *principle* is therefore stable, and a "typical 19th-century" head is faithful to Jacquard's design.

### Gaps
- The hook count and card size of the first machine. No primary source I read gives them. Look at the *Dictionnaire technologique* vol. XI plate (Gallica) and the CNAM object, if it is the original (see §3).
- The size of the original frame and how high it stood above the loom.
- Kohl's printed page numbers are read from OCR page markers (pp.11–12, 32–34) and should be checked against the scan.

## 3. Surviving, reconstructed and demonstrated machines

### Takeaway
Many 19th- and 20th-century Jacquard looms survive with good online records, above all in the Science Museum Group (CC0 data, CC BY text, measurements). **No online catalogue record of "Jacquard's own machine" was found.** CNAM's Jacquard-era object(s) have no reachable online record. The secondary sources disagree on whether CNAM shows a model or an 1804 loom. Working Jacquard hand looms are demonstrated at the Maison des Canuts (Lyon) and have been at Paisley Museum. The Science and Industry Museum (Manchester) displays its loom, but I found no current demonstration schedule.

## 4. The woven portrait, Lovelace's Note A, Babbage's cards and Hollerith

### Takeaway
The woven silk portrait (Didier, Petit et Cie, shown at the 1839 Paris exposition, woven by Michel-Marie Carquillat from a mise en carte by J.-L. Moulin) is well documented, and copies are in at least five museums. The "24,000 cards" figure is repeated everywhere, but none of the primary or museum-catalogue sources I opened gives it. Lovelace's "weaves algebraical patterns" line is in Note A, *Scientific Memoirs* vol. 3 (1843). Babbage owned and showed a copy of the portrait.

### Cited findings

**The woven portrait.**
- The painting is by Jean-Claude Bonnefond for Lyon's Hôtel de Ville (Musée des Beaux-Arts, Lyon, inv. A 137). An approval sketch is in the Musée des Tissus, inv. MT 2014.0.28 [MDT-2014-DP].
- Didier-Petit et Cie showed the woven version at the "Exposition des produits français de l'industrie à Paris en 1839" ("Paris exhibition of French industrial products, 1839"). Its painting was "peint cinq ans auparavant" ("painted five years earlier").
- The woven version credits only Didier-Petit and Bonnefond. Its real makers were the metteur en carte (pattern draughtsman) **J.-L. Moulin** and the weaver **Michel-Marie Carquillat**. Moulin worked "deux mois et demi" ("two and a half months") on the mise en carte, helped by Joseph-Victor Vibert. The original mise en carte "est aujourd'hui perdue" ("is now lost"). Moulin's own partial copy (upper part) is in the Musée des Tissus, inv. MT 25800, "papier réglé de 10 en 10" ("paper ruled in tens"). Each line is read in turn to punch the cards ("le lisage", card reading) [MDT-2014-DP].
- Musée des Tissus exhibition "Jacquard, point à la ligne", 7 Nov 2014 – 4 Jan 2015 [MDT-2014-DP].
- Copies:
  - Science Museum 1942-59, Lyons 1839: "in frame 20" x 14", frame 31" x 27""; W 588 × H 708 × D 29 mm [SMG-1942-59].
  - CHM X341.84, c. 1839, Didier Petit & Co, gift of Gwen and Gordon Bell [CHM-X341.84].
  - Metropolitan Museum 222531 (1839; page not opened, 429 error).
  - NMAH 622346 (not on view, snippet).
  - The Henry Ford 39.173.1 (snippet).
  - Science History Institute (acquired about 2015) [SHI-2015].
  - The CHM blog lists further copies at the Art Institute of Chicago and the Met [CHM-BLOG-2018].
- **24,000 cards:** the Science History Institute says "more than 24,000 cards" and "approximately 24,000" [SHI-2015]. The CHM blog quotes a donor saying "24,000 punched cards" without confirming it [CHM-BLOG-2018]. Wikipedia's caption gives no citation [WIKI-JACQUARD-MACHINE]. The SMG, CHM and Musée des Tissus records do not give a card count.
- Dates: the CHM blog calls it "an 1830 woven portrait" and also says "ca. 1839" [CHM-BLOG-2018]. 1830 is wrong, since the painting dates from 1833–34 [MDT-2014-DP].

**Babbage and the portrait** [BABBAGE-1864]:
- Babbage had the portrait "hanging up in my drawing-room". Showing it to Prince Albert and the Duke of Wellington, Wellington took it for an engraving. It was "a sheet of woven silk, framed and glazed, but looking so perfectly like an engraving, that it had been mistaken for such by two members of the Royal Academy". Wilkie guessed line engraving, then lithography [BABBAGE-1864 pp.169–170].
- In a footnote about taking it to show a king, the portrait's dimensions "were 2 ft. 8 in. by 2 ft. 2 in." [BABBAGE-1864 p.306–307]. This is larger than SMG's 20 × 14 in image size, perhaps because it includes margins or the inscription panel. *Unresolved.*
- In December 1839 Babbage wrote to François Arago about getting a copy. By 1842 he was showing it to guests [SHI-2015].
- Babbage explains the Engine through the Jacquard loom: "It is known as a fact that the Jacquard loom is capable of weaving any design which the imagination of man may conceive" [BABBAGE-1864 p.116–117].

**Babbage adopts cards.**
- Babbage moved the Analytical Engine to Jacquard cards in mid-1836, replacing studded barrels. A notebook entry of 1836 (often dated 30 June) adds repeating and card punching. Bromley says a workable design existed by mid-1836, with a major revision in late 1837 (snippets) [BABBAGE-1836].
- **Not confirmed:** the exact 30 June date. Only secondary snippets were seen.

**Lovelace, Note A.**
- Menabrea's "Sketch of the Analytical Engine", translated with "Notes by the Translator" (A.A.L.), *Scientific Memoirs* vol. 3 (London: R. & J. E. Taylor, 1843), pp.666–731 [LOVELACE-1843].
- The passage: "The distinctive characteristic of the Analytical Engine … is the introduction into it of the principle which Jacquard devised for regulating, by means of punched cards, the most complicated patterns in the fabrication of brocaded stuffs. … We may say most aptly, that the Analytical Engine *weaves algebraical patterns* just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves" [LOVELACE-1843, Note A] ("weaves algebraical patterns" is in italics in the fourmilab text).
- Page:
  - In the Taylor offprint (Gutenberg #75107), Note A begins on offprint p.26 and the quote is on p.30.
  - With the offprint's p.1 equal to *Scientific Memoirs* p.666, that is **SM p.691 (Note A begins) and p.696 (quote)**. This is my calculation.
  - It agrees with a Yale transcription that cites "Note A, p.696" [YALE-LOVELACE (snippet)].
- The Science and Industry Museum blog misquotes it as "weaves algebraic patterns, just as the Jacquard loom weaves…" [SIM-BLOG-2017]. Use the 1843 wording.

**Hollerith.**
- Hollerith's own story credits a conductor punching tickets: a railway conductor in most versions, a tram conductor in Columbia's.
- Historians add influences: John Shaw Billings' suggestion, telegraph and library practice, and "probably" Jacquard looms. "Hollerith had no knowledge of the work of Charles Babbage" (snippets) [HOLLERITH].
- The cards were used for the 1890 US census [WIKI-JACQUARD-MACHINE].

### Inferences
- The simulator can show the portrait story and the Lovelace quote with full citations. The card count should be shown as "about 24,000 (traditional figure; no primary source found)".
- A direct line from Jacquard through Babbage to Hollerith is too simple. Babbage borrowed the cards on purpose. Hollerith's debt is indirect and may not exist.

### Gaps
- An 1839–40 source for the card count. The Didier-Petit exposition catalogue and the 1839 jury report are not searched.
- Babbage's 1836 notebook text (Science Museum Babbage papers) was not opened.

## 5. Recommended reference machine

### Takeaway
Model **a typical 19th-century single-lift, 8-row Jacquard head of about 400 hooks (plus 2 reserve rows), with a square four-faced cylinder, on a wooden silk hand loom worked by one treadle.** Take the Science Museum's Spitalfields loom 1914-469 (400 needles, head c. 1820–34) as the physical reference, and Barlow 1878 and Posselt 1888 as the drawings. Label the model as "the Jacquard machine as used from c. 1810s to 1900", not "Jacquard's 1804 prototype".

### Cited findings (basis)
- 400 needles was "an ordinary 400-needle machine — such as is in common use in silk hand-loom weaving", and hand-loom heads were "usually made of wood" [BARLOW-1878 p.149].
- A measured, catalogued surviving example exists: 1914-469, 400 needles, 2800 × 1630 × 3050 mm, CC0 data with CC BY text [SMG-1914-469].
- Full-dimension drawings of a head (200-hook, ¼ scale) give every part size. Scaling the 26 columns × 8 rows to 400 hooks is the obvious step (see the inference below) [POSSELT-1888].
- The principle was unchanged from the original form: 8 knives, square prism, lantern and catches, brass springs, two reserve rows [KOHL-1873 pp.32–33].
- The 1804–05 original has **no** surviving catalogued object reachable online and **no** known hook count [§2, §3].

### Inferences
- **Why not the 1804 original:**
  - Its hook count, card size and frame size are unknown.
  - The CNAM object may be a model.
  - Its patent status is disputed.
  - Breton's 1815 changes, which made it reliable, are undocumented in detail.
  - Modelling it would mean inventing details and presenting them as fact.
- **Why 400 hooks, single lift:**
  - It was the period's standard hand-loom size.
  - It is small enough to draw every hook (8 × 50 plus 2 × 8 reserve is my assumed layout; check it against the 1914-469 head or photos).
  - It is large enough to weave a recognisable figure.
  - Single lift is the simplest mechanism to explain (one griffe, one hook per needle).
  - Double lift [POSSELT-1888] and Verdol paper [ROJAS-2012] can come later as "versions".
- **Teaching path:**
  - A 2D or 3D lineage view: Bouchon's paper band, then Falcon's card chain on a square prism, then Vaucanson's drum and griffe, then the Jacquard head.
  - The CNAM Vaucanson loom (330 × 266 × 277 cm) and the SMG Falcon model (950 × 500 × 1030 mm) give real sizes for those side views.
- **Card dimensions** for a 400 head must be measured. Posselt gives a 1/4-scale 208-hole card drawing, and the Science Museum holds 19th-century cards (co471243, co503962) [SMG-SEARCH].

### Gaps
- The actual row × column layout and card size of SMG 1914-469. Ask SMG, or measure from photos (the images are CC BY-NC-SA).
- A drawing of the treadle–lever–griffe linkage on a hand loom with dimensions. Barlow's figures 128–131 and Posselt's Figs. XII–XIII (a 400 head by W. P. Uhlinger, lever from below) are candidates but were not measured.

## 6. Popular claims checked

| Claim | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| "Jacquard patented his loom in 1801" | **Misleading.** The 1801 patent (23 Dec 1801) covers an earlier, pedal-type "lacs-drawing" machine. No patent for the card machine was found by Kohl. | KOHL-1873; SEARCH-BREVET-1801 (snippets) |
| "Invented 1804" | **Conventional.** The likely date is 1805, with first sales and premiums in 1806. 1804 is when he returned to Lyon. | KOHL-1873; SMG-1867-47 |
| "First programmable machine" / "first punch-card machine" | **Popular, not supported.** Bouchon's paper (1725) and Falcon's cards (1728) came first. SMG calls Falcon's loom "the first machine of any kind to be controlled by punch cards". Barrel-programmed automata and organs are older still (not researched here). | BARLOW-1878; SMG-1963-379 |
| "Jacquard invented the punched card" | **False.** Barlow: "It is generally taken for granted that he invented the application of perforated cards" but the Marin models show Bouchon and Falcon first. | BARLOW-1878 p.140–141 |
| "Portrait woven with 24,000 cards" | **Traditional, unverified.** Repeated by SHI and CHM, but no 1839 or museum-record source. | SHI-2015; CHM-BLOG-2018 |
| "His machine was publicly burned" | **Disputed.** Jacquard (via Bowring) says it was broken up and sold for scrap. Rojas says the burning "n'a jamais existé" ("never happened"). | BARLOW-1878 p.145; ROJAS-2012 |
| "CNAM holds Jacquard's original 1804 loom" | **Unconfirmed.** Rojas describes a *maquette* (model); Larousse and other snippets say an 1804 loom. | ROJAS-2012; LAROUSSE |
| Lovelace: "weaves algebraic patterns" | **Misquote.** The original reads "weaves algebraical patterns … Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves". | LOVELACE-1843 |
| Hollerith copied Jacquard | **Overstated.** He told of a conductor's ticket punch; Jacquard is a "probable" influence; he did not know Babbage's work. | HOLLERITH (snippets) |
